The climate cost of conflict

War carries a climate cost that extends far beyond the battlefield and persists long after the fighting ends.

Researchers estimate that militaries account for around 5.5 per cent of global greenhouse gas emissions, before counting the impact of fighting itself.

Yet emissions are only part of the picture. Before a war starts, the defence industry consumes vast natural resources to manufacture weapons, build fortifications and move troops and equipment. Once fighting begins, war destroys forests and other carbon stores, damages water systems and farmland, disrupts climate adaptation and diverts public money from climate action. When it ends, debris, unexploded munitions and toxic waste leave pollutants in soil and water for years. Rebuilding then adds further emissions.

We often hear how climate change drives conflict. Far less is known about how conflict damages the climate. The New Humanitarian is bringing this hidden toll into view. Our project combines data, on-the-ground reporting and conversations among climate, humanitarian and peacebuilding experts to show what conflict is doing to the planet and to the people living with the consequences.

What we bring

  • Data and human stories, together. Bringing data and evidence together with first-hand accounts from affected communities.
  • Beyond the headline wars. Most research focuses on a few high-profile conflicts. We draw on our reporting from crises that rarely make the news.
  • Beyond carbon. We look at the harms that outlast the fighting: poisoned water, ruined farmland, displacement and land left unusable for years.
  • Convening expertise across disciplines and regions. Evidence and expertise do not consistently reach across sectors, leaving the wider consequences of conflict poorly understood. We bring actors together to show what we know, what remains unknown and where action is most needed.
